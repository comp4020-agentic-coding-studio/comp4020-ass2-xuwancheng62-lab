# Thinking in shots

Week 4 — SLOP4555

---

## A scene is one thing. A sequence is many decisions.

On the page, a scene is one thing happening. On screen, it's a sequence of
decisions about what the audience is allowed to see, and when.

This week turns last week's page into that sequence.

---

## The shot vocabulary — and what each one is *for*

| Shot | What it's for |
|---|---|
| Wide | place, distance, isolation |
| Medium | body language, relation between two people |
| Close-up | a decision happening on a face |
| Insert | one detail the story needs noticed |

Not variety for its own sake — every shot has to earn its place.

---

## Composition and angle are an argument

Where the frame's edge falls says something about power, distance,
intimacy. Blocking and camera movement are one decision, not two: where a
body moves and where the camera moves have to agree.

---

## Mamet's test: the shot as argument

Build the list through *juxtaposition*, not performance, narration, or
camera movement. Mamet's own example: cut face → cut fridge interior
argues "hunger" — no line needed.

The filter for every shot: **what does the character want in this moment,
and will the audience see whether they get it, from this shot next to this
one?**

A shot list's purposes can't repeat — two shots with the same purpose means
one of them is a safety copy, not an argument.

---

## Five shots, one purpose each

| Shot | Type | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Wide | how alone she is with the decision |
| 2 | Insert (screen) | audience learns the name with her |
| 3 | Close-up (still face) | she's seen it, isn't moving yet |
| 4 | Insert (hand) | the hesitation is the whole frame |
| 5 | Close-up, tilts with her hand to her face | acceptance and the line as one motion |

No repeated purposes — each cut argues something the last shot hadn't yet.

---

## Guided judgement: which pairing argues something?

Beat: reading a text, setting it face-down — while a housemate's visible in
the background. Intention: **concealment**, not just finishing a text.

- Medium (housemate visible) → wide of the room: repeats what's already
  shown. Coverage.
- Close-up (housemate drops out of frame) → insert of the phone, face-down:
  visualises the hiding. Argument.

Scoped to this beat, not a rule against wide shots — shot 1 above is a wide,
and it's doing real work.

---

## The first storyboard: a legible thumbnail

A frame, a rough figure, an arrow for movement. It doesn't need to be good —
it needs to be legible enough that someone else could stage the shot from it
without asking you anything.

## "Before It Stops"

Breaking the closing scene into a shot sequence — her hand drifting toward
the phone, stopping, then closing over it on the final ring as the camera
tilts up with her hand to her face for "Hey": which cut argues the
hesitation, and which ones just get you there.

Not every shot in a sequence is equally important — the shot list is where
you decide which one is.

---

## This week's task

Break your scene into a numbered shot list — type, purpose, one line each —
then sketch a rough thumbnail under every entry. Read the list to a partner
without showing them the scene. If they can't tell what a shot is doing from
the number, purpose and thumbnail alone, merge or cut it.
