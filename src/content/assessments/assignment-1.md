## The brief

> Direct the film on paper before you direct it in generation.

Submit the complete pre-production package for the **2–4 minute dramatic
short** you will make in the second half of the course. Integrate the story,
screenplay, continuity design and shot planning developed in Weeks 2–6.
Your package should let another filmmaker understand what happens, what each
shot contributes, and what must remain consistent between generations.

You are being assessed on directing decisions made before production.
Generated footage is not required. Rough hand-drawn or digital storyboard
thumbnails are sufficient; their staging must be readable.

## What you submit

Submit **one PDF** with your name, film title, package version/date and these
four labelled sections. Embed any references you rely on in the PDF, with
captions and version labels, so the reader does not need access to your
private folders or accounts. There is no fixed page count or shot count:
include what your whole film needs.

1. **Logline and premise** — the locked story and its scope.
2. **Screenplay and director's intent notes** — the complete film in screenplay form.
3. **Character and world bible** — repeatable identity, design and continuity decisions.
4. **Shot list, storyboard and production order** — every shot, its purpose and dependencies.

“Locked” means the submitted sections agree with one another, and every
decision is either resolved or explicitly marked pending with what's needed
to close it. A deliberate later revision is possible;
update the affected documents together rather than allowing them to drift.

## Work through the package: “Before It Stops”

The course example follows Nadia on the final night before her late mother's
apartment is cleared. Her estranged brother Theo calls. She hesitates, then
answers on the final ring and says “Hey.” She chooses contact; the film does
not claim their relationship is repaired.

Use the examples below to understand the decisions, then make them for
**your own film**. These are authored teaching drafts, not a complete
submission or evidence of a tested production. The current five-shot
screenplay plan estimates **24–38 seconds**, short of this assignment's
full runtime requirement. Nadia's vocal design is left pending as a worked
example of that taught state, not a defect — but a short runtime is not:
a full submission needs a developed screenplay reaching 2–4 minutes, not
padded holds or additional angles added only to reach that length.

### 1. Lock the logline and premise

**Your task:** Write a one-sentence logline naming the protagonist, want,
obstacle and stakes. Then write a one-paragraph premise explaining the
starting situation, how the conflict becomes visible and the ending. These
are working production descriptions: include the ending rather than writing
a teaser. Check that the story can unfold in 2–4 minutes.

**Example logline:**

> On the final night before her late mother's apartment is cleared, Nadia
> wants to answer her estranged brother's call but must overcome her own
> restraint after years of silence, or let this chance for contact pass when
> the ringing stops.

**Example premise:**

> On the final night before her late mother's apartment is cleared, Nadia
> finishes packing the kitchen and sits alone at its table when her estranged
> brother Theo calls. She wants to answer, but holds herself back from
> speaking to someone she has avoided for years. As the phone continues to
> ring, reaching for it and withdrawing make the conflict visible: she can
> keep their silence intact, or risk making contact while the call is still
> there to answer. On the last ring before the call would go to voicemail,
> she finally accepts and says “Hey”, then listens. The moment ends with her
> choosing contact, before either sibling has to resolve their years of
> estrangement.

**Check your work:** Underline the want, obstacle and stakes. Identify what
we will see or hear that makes the conflict readable. “A woman remembers her
family” leaves those decisions to the reader; Nadia's interrupted reach gives
them an action to stage.

### 2. Write the whole screenplay and explain your intent

**Your task:** Cover the film's beginning to its ending — whether that's one
continuous scene or several. Use scene headings naming interior/exterior,
location and time of day;
observable action; and character cues and dialogue where needed. A silent
film does not need dialogue added to satisfy the format. Add intent notes
that name the relevant beat, the intended audience reading and the action or
staging that supports it. Keep explanations in the notes and filmable action
in the screenplay.

In the example screenplay, the kitchen is
established through bare shelves, packed boxes and Nadia sealing the last
box. The decisive sequence then consists of:

1. The phone lights up with **THEO CALLING**.
2. Nadia sees the name and remains still.
3. Her hand reaches toward the phone and stops.
4. On the final ring, she accepts and says **“Hey.”**
5. She listens with the phone at her ear; fade out.

**Example screenplay excerpt:**

```text
INT. MOTHER'S APARTMENT - KITCHEN - NIGHT

Her phone lights up beside it: THEO CALLING.

She looks at the screen. She does not move.

The phone keeps ringing.

Her hand drifts toward it. Stops.

One ring left before it would go to voicemail.

Her hand closes over the phone. She answers.

                              NADIA
                    Hey.
```

**Worked intent note:** At the reach-and-stop beat, the audience should see
that Nadia wants contact but is holding herself back. Let the hand stop
short of touching the phone while the ringtone continues. In the final
beat, answering is a risk she takes; hold her listening rather than adding
a smile or Theo's reply that would imply reconciliation.

This note connects intent to a visible decision. “Make it emotional” does
not tell another filmmaker what to direct.

**Check your work:** Can every story change in the premise be found in an
observable screenplay beat? Does the ending remain the same? Have you written
the complete film rather than only the scene practised in Week 3?

### 3. Make the bible repeatable

**Your task:** Give each character, location and story-relevant prop an entry
labelled for use in the shot list. Record the concrete details needed to
reproduce it, any selected reference and its version, and what must stay
fixed. Record changing state separately. Costume belongs with the character
or in a clearly linked costume entry. For voices actually heard in the film,
record a voice entry — a reference and repeatable vocal qualities — or mark
it pending with the decision needed to close it, the same way Week 5
modelled; do not invent an appearance for someone never shown.

| Entry | Fixed identity/design in the teaching draft | Planned changing state |
|---|---|---|
| Nadia | In her thirties; short brown bob; oatmeal chunky knit sweater and dark trousers | Looks at the phone, reaches and stops, then accepts, lifts it and speaks |
| Theo | Nadia's estranged brother, represented by the caller text **THEO CALLING** | Incoming call becomes accepted; no shot of Theo or spoken reply is specified |
| Kitchen | Honey-coloured wooden table, cream cabinets, dark nighttime window, warm pendant light and packing boxes | Nadia finishes packing and settles; the phone moves from table to ear |
| Phone | The same black smartphone and readable caller identity throughout | Screen-up on the table while ringing; accepted and lifted to Nadia's ear in the final shot |

Nadia's voice is left **pending** here, exactly as Week 5 modelled — name
the decision still needed rather than leaving the field blank. Theo's
on-screen appearance is different: it is **not applicable**, because he is
never shown.

**Check your work:** Could a reader reproduce the same person, costume,
space and prop? “Keep the phone in the same place” fails because the ending
requires it to move. “Keep the same phone; table before acceptance, ear after
the lift” preserves identity while allowing the action.

### 4. Plan every shot and storyboard its staging

**Your task:** List shots in **screen order**, using unique IDs shared with
the storyboard. For each shot, record its scene/beat, distinct purpose,
framing, angle, fixed camera or movement, subject action and dependencies.
Your drawings should show subject placement, important props, eyelines and
movement where relevant. Stick figures and arrows are enough if another
reader can stage the shot.

The current single-scene teaching draft uses five shots:

| ID | Framing/action | What this shot contributes |
|---|---|---|
| 01 | Fixed eye-level wide: Nadia seals the last box, settles, phone lights up | Establish the imminent departure surrounding her decision |
| 02 | Static phone insert: **THEO CALLING**, no hand touching it | Identify the estranged brother as the caller |
| 03 | Fixed face close-up: she looks toward the phone and stays still | Make her withholding a response visible |
| 04 | Static hand/phone insert: reach, stop short of contact | Turn hesitation into an interrupted attempt at contact |
| 05 | One continuous shot: accept and grasp, tilt up with the lift, “Hey,” listen | Complete the choice of contact while leaving its outcome unresolved |

This table summarises the example. In your own package, expand each row
with the full required shot fields and a matching storyboard thumbnail.

**Example staging:** In shot 04, frame Nadia's right hand, the tabletop and
the phone on screen-right. Draw an arrow toward the phone and mark the gap
where her fingers stop. In shot 05, begin at that same stopped position;
show the acceptance and lift, then the phone at her right ear. Indicate the
camera's upward tilt separately from the hand's movement.

Shot 05 needs multiple storyboard stages: tabletop acceptance, lift, and
face/phone at the ear. Label them **05A, 05B, 05C — stages of shot 05, no cuts**.
A continuous camera tilt must not become three different shots by accident.
Keep the hand, phone position and eyeline consistent across shots 03–05.
The ringtone continues until acceptance, then stops.

**Check your work:** Match every screenplay beat to a shot ID. Match every
shot ID to its storyboard. Taken together, does your shot list plausibly
cover a 120–240 second film? There is no minimum number of shots. The
example's short screenplay is a scope gap, not a reason to stretch the
ringtone.

### 5. Check dependencies and assemble the final package

Within section 4, add a **production order** referencing the existing shot
IDs. Screen order tells us how the film will be watched; production order
tells us which resolved designs and references must be available before each
shot can be attempted. Name those dependencies and mark each shot ready only
when the required decisions are locked.

For “Before It Stops,” shot 02 needs the phone/display design; shot 03 needs
Nadia's face/costume and eyeline; shot 05 needs Nadia, the phone, her “Hey”
vocal design and the planned continuous lift/tilt. Its start must match shot
04's stopped hand. Where a design decision like this is still pending, mark
shot 05 blocked on it rather than scheduling it as ready — the same
dependency discipline Week 6 taught. No actual generation is required to
write this plan.

Finally, give the package to an unfamiliar reader and ask them to describe
the story, character/prop identities and staging using only the documents.
Use the Week 5 read-back and Week 6 presentation methods to find gaps.
Resolve every decision you can, and make sure any field still pending names
the specific decision needed to close it; fix any contradictory
instructions before submission. This is a practical completion check; a
separate peer-review report is not an additional submission component.

## How the marking criteria apply

The three criteria retain their published weights. The spec establishes
what must be present; these criteria judge how well the decisions work.

| Criterion | What the marker looks for |
|---|---|
| **Story and screenplay craft — 30%** | A clear want, obstacle and stakes; a filmable change expressed through action; a complete, feasible screenplay; specific intent notes; agreement between story descriptions and ending |
| **World and continuity design — 30%** | Concrete repeatable identities and geography; design choices resolved where possible and honestly, specifically justified where pending; usable labelled references where relied on; a clear distinction between fixed facts and planned state changes |
| **Shot planning and storyboard — 40%** | Complete beat coverage; shots with distinct contributions; readable staging and movement; coherent continuity; a runtime the shot list plausibly supports and a dependency-aware production order |

A complete package lets the reader stage your choices without filling in
missing decisions. Strong work also makes clear why those choices serve the
film: why this action, this frame and this ending.

## Detailed marking rubric

Each criterion above splits into three dimensions. Each dimension is scored
out of 10; a criterion's mark is its weight scaled by the average of its
three dimension scores (for example, three dimension scores of 8, 7 and 9 —
average 8 — earn 40 × 8/10 = 32 of the 40 marks available for Shot planning
and storyboard). The bands below describe what earns each range; a score
can fall between bands where a piece of work only partly matches a
description.

### Story and screenplay craft (30%)

| Dimension | 7–10 | 5–6 | 1–4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Want, obstacle and stakes | The logline, premise and screenplay all play out a specific want, a concrete obstacle and an immediate stake in observable action, not narration | Want, obstacle and stakes are present but at least one is vague, or only partly visible in the screenplay's actual beats | Want, obstacle or stakes are missing, generic, or contradicted by what the screenplay actually shows |
| Screenplay completeness | The screenplay covers the whole film beginning to end, correctly formatted (headings, observable action, dialogue where used), with a runtime estimate the described action justifies | The screenplay is mostly complete and correctly formatted, but one scene or beat is thin or missing, or the runtime doesn't match the action described | The screenplay is fragmentary (for example, only the Week 3 practice scene), misformatted, or its runtime is unjustified or padded |
| Director's intent notes | Every note names a concrete antecedent circumstance or visible action tied to a specific beat, not a mood word or a prescribed result | Most notes are specific, but one or two lapse into result-oriented adjectives ("more dramatic") or aren't tied to a specific beat | Notes are missing, generic, or describe an emotional result rather than a directable action or circumstance |

### World and continuity design (30%)

| Dimension | 7–10 | 5–6 | 1–4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repeatable identity | Every character, location and story-relevant prop has an entry with concrete, reproducible detail, cleanly separated from changing action or state | Entries exist for most elements, but at least one lacks enough concrete detail to reproduce, or mixes fixed identity with changing state | Entries are missing for elements the screenplay or shots actually use, or are too vague to reproduce ("a woman in her thirties," nothing further) |
| Resolved vs. pending decisions | Every bible decision the shots need is either resolved or explicitly marked pending with the exact decision still needed; references are labelled and versioned | Most decisions are resolved or clearly pending, but one field is blank, unlabelled, or pending without saying what would close it | Decisions are left unresolved without being marked pending, or references are unlabelled and unversioned, leaving a reader unable to tell what's settled |
| Cross-document continuity | Story, ending, character identity, prop state and spatial continuity agree exactly across the logline, premise, screenplay, bible, shot list and storyboard | One inconsistency exists between documents (a prop's state, a location detail) that a careful reader would need to reconcile | Multiple contradictions exist across the documents, or a major element — the ending, the protagonist's identity — disagrees between sections |

### Shot planning and storyboard (40%)

| Dimension | 7–10 | 5–6 | 1–4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beat coverage and shot purpose | Every screenplay beat maps to a shot ID in screen order, and each shot's stated purpose is distinct from every other shot's | Coverage is mostly complete, but one beat lacks a shot, or two shots share the same stated purpose | Shots skip significant beats, or most shots share a generic purpose ("cover the action") rather than a distinct contribution |
| Staging legibility and continuity | Every shot specifies framing, angle, movement and subject action; storyboard thumbnails share matching IDs, are legible enough to stage without guessing, and add extra stages where one frame can't show the movement | Most shots are fully specified and storyboarded, but one or two thumbnails are unclear, missing a needed extra stage, or a field is missing | Several shots lack a storyboard thumbnail, fields are missing across most shots, or the drawings don't show enough to stage the shot |
| Timing and production order | The shot list, taken as a whole, plausibly covers the required 2–4 minute runtime; a production order names each shot's dependencies and correctly flags any shot blocked by a pending decision | The shot list's overall runtime is close to plausible for 2–4 minutes, and a production order exists but misses a dependency or doesn't correctly flag one blocked shot | The shot list's runtime is implausible or clearly padded to reach the range; there is no production order, or dependencies aren't tracked at all |
