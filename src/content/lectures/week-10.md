The same shots, cut two different ways, tell two different stories about
what a character feels. Nothing gets regenerated this week — the footage from

## What you'll be able to do

- Treat a cut point as a directorial decision about what the audience
  notices next, not a technical seam between two shots.
- Use shot duration — holding on a reaction versus cutting away from it —
  as a deliberate emotional instrument.
- Recut the same sequence twice with a different pacing decision each
  time, and describe the emotional difference each produces.

The lecture runs 90 minutes: recap 5, outcomes 5, technique and worked
recut 45, comparison 15, judgement practice 15, synthesis 5.

## A cut is an argument, not a seam

Week 4 and week 8 both used David Mamet's idea that a shot argues
something — no two shots in a sequence should make the same argument. The
same frame extends to where the cut between them falls. A cut point
decides what the audience is allowed to keep watching and what it's asked
to leave behind; two different cut points on identical footage make two
different claims about what the scene is about, without changing a single
generated frame. Editing this week isn't decoration applied after the
shots exist — it's where a second layer of directorial argument gets made,
entirely in the arrangement.

## Pacing and shot duration as instruments

Holding on a reaction and cutting away from it early are opposite
directorial choices, not a technical preference about rhythm. Holding
places the audience inside the feeling — it argues the feeling is the
point of the scene. Cutting away denies the audience that feeling on
purpose — it argues something else is the point, and the reaction is
secondary to it. Neither choice is more correct in general; each is
correct or wrong only against what the sequence is supposed to be about.

## Montage and rhythm

A single long take can only show one thing happening at one pace.
Repetition and juxtaposition — cutting between separate moments, or
returning to the same image more than once — do something a long take
can't: they compress time, build an association between two things that
never shared a frame, or let two moments comment on each other without a
line of dialogue saying so. That's still an argument, made through
arrangement rather than through what's inside any one shot.

more time with her stillness or an earlier move into her attempt to act.

## Misconception contrast

| Treating a cut as a technical seam | Treating a cut as an argument |
|---|---|
| "Where does this shot naturally end?" | "What does the audience notice first because of where I cut?" |
| Duration set by instinct or habit | Duration set by what feeling the hold or cut-away is meant to produce |
| A recut is just a different arrangement | A recut is a different claim about what the scene is about |
| More cuts reads as more dynamic | A cut's timing is judged against the argument it's making |

## Guided judgement: what does this cut argue?

A student cuts the same five shots so that shot 2 (the phone insert) is
held for nearly twice as long as shot 3 (her still face), before her reaction appears. Identify what that pacing decision argues about the
scene, before checking the diagnosis.

<details>
<summary>Compare your diagnosis</summary>

Holding the phone insert longer than her reaction emphasizes the phone —
the interruption itself — while giving her response less screen time.
This is a different choice from the actual Cut B above, which keeps the
phone insert unchanged and moves sooner from her reaction into the reach. That
might be exactly the right choice for a version of this film about an
unwelcome intrusion; it would be the wrong choice for a version about her
interior conflict, which needs the opposite ratio, closer to Cut A. Neither
duration is right in isolation — the diagnosis is whether it matches what
the sequence is supposed to be about.

</details>

For the workshop, apply the same test to your own two cuts before writing
the sentence each spec bullet asks for: what does each cut's pacing argue,
and does that match what you intended the scene to be about? One of your
two cuts should be this kind of duration decision; the other should use
the montage move above — repeating or reordering a shot — rather than a
second duration change.
