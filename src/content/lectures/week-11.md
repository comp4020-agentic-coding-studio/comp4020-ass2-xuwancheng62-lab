A film with picture locked and no sound pass is still a rough cut, no matter
how finished the images look. Sound is where the last third of the audience's
attention actually lives.

## What you'll be able to do

- Direct a voice performance with the same single-note discipline used on
  a face — a note aimed at pace, breath, or hesitation, not a mood word.
- Treat a location's sound signature as a second bible entry, fixed and
  checked for continuity the same way a visual reference is.
- Place a music cue as a directorial statement about how a moment should
  feel, and hear precisely what it adds by comparing a cut with and
  without it.

The lecture runs 90 minutes: recap 5, outcomes 5, technique and worked
comparison 45, comparison 15, judgement practice 15, synthesis 5.

## Directing a voice like a face

Week 1 directed a line reading with a single note aimed at something
concrete and playable — not "sadder," but a specific change in pace,
breath, or where a hesitation lands. That same discipline applies
unchanged to a voice generated on its own, independent of the face it will
eventually sync to. "More emotional" fails as a voice note for the same
reason it failed as a performance note in week 1: it names a mood, not
something a take can be judged against.

Directing a line reading also rewards a kind of responsiveness Sanford
Meisner's actor training names as "living truthfully under imaginary
circumstances" — treating the moment as something being lived through in
real time, rather than a pre-decided delivery played back. Framing that as
a technique specifically *for directors* is this course's own inference,
not documented Meisner-for-directors pedagogy — no Meisner-authored or
Institute-published curriculum for directors was found in preparing this
course. What transfers cleanly, regardless of that framing's provenance,
is the practical note: direct the moment being lived, not the line being
recited.

## Ambience and Foley as a location's second bible

Week 5 established that a location bible entry has to be checked, not
remembered from scene to scene, because nothing about a generated
location persists on its own. Sound has the identical problem. What a
kitchen at night sounds like — the low hum of an empty room, a tap
dripping, traffic outside — is as fixed a fact about that location as its
layout or its lighting, and it needs to be locked and re-supplied the same
way, or a sequence's ambience will drift exactly as its visuals would
without a bible to check against.

## Music as a directorial statement, not wallpaper

A music cue laid under a whole scene by default isn't a directorial
decision — it's the absence of one. Placed on purpose, a cue is a specific
claim about how a moment should feel, the same category of choice as a
cut point in week 10 or a shot's argument in week 4 and week 8. Removing
the cue doesn't just make the moment quieter — it removes a claim the
scene was making, and the difference is worth hearing directly rather than
assuming.

## Misconception contrast

| Sound as a finishing layer | Sound as a directed layer |
|---|---|
| A line note aimed at "more emotional" | A note aimed at pace, breath, or hesitation |
| Ambience however the room happens to render | A location's sound signature locked like its visual bible |
| Music placed under the whole scene by default | A cue placed on purpose, with a stated claim |
| "It feels more finished" as the only judgement | Naming what changed emotionally, beat by beat |

## Guided judgement: what is this cue actually doing?

A student adds a swelling string cue under the entire closing shot,
starting the instant the phone lights up and continuing through "Hey."
Identify what claim that placement makes, and whether it matches the
beat's intent from week 7's moment-before.

<details>
<summary>Compare your diagnosis</summary>

Starting the cue the instant the phone lights up claims the *anticipation*
is the emotional center of the beat — the swell arrives well before the
directed hesitation from week 7 does any work, which risks announcing the
outcome before her performance has earned it. If the beat's intent is that
her hesitation, grounded in the call interrupting her final packing
task in her mother's kitchen, is what the moment is actually about, a cue entering later — under
the reach itself, not the first ring — matches that intent more precisely.
The fix isn't to remove the cue; it's to move where its claim starts.

</details>

For the workshop, apply the same test to your own cue placement: what
claim does the moment it starts on make, and does that match what the beat
is actually about?
