Shots generated separately need to look like they happened in the same room,
on the same night. This week is about noticing exactly where they don't, and
fixing only that — not regenerating a sequence that's already mostly right.

## What you'll be able to do

- Name what a sequence of separately generated shots must match, and what
  small variance the eye simply forgives.
- Watch a cut cold and log continuity failures as specific findings, not a
  vague impression that "something's off."
- Decide, for each finding, whether to fix or accept it, and give the
  reason — then repair only the shot the log actually flagged.

The lecture runs 90 minutes: recap 5, outcomes 5, technique and worked log
45, comparison 15, judgement practice 15, synthesis 5.

## What has to match, and what the eye forgives

Every shot in "Before It Stops" so far was generated on its own, from its
own anchor and its own note. Nothing guarantees they agree with each other
unless someone checks. Some disagreement matters and some doesn't:

| Has to match | The eye forgives |
|---|---|
| Wardrobe and prop position between shots of the same moment | Small lighting drift between separately generated takes |
| Geography — where the table, the door, the box stack sit | A slightly different lens feel shot to shot |
| Screen direction — which way she's facing relative to the phone | Minor skin-tone variance |

The distinction matters because fixing what the eye already forgives wastes
a regeneration, the same waste week 6 flagged for scheduling ahead of a
pending reference, and week 8 flagged for rewriting a note that wasn't
actually the problem.

## Recognising continuity failures precisely

"It looks a bit off" isn't a finding — it's the same shapeless complaint as
"more dramatic" in week 7 or "make it better" in week 8, and it's just as
undirectable. A usable finding names the thing: *character drift* (a prop,
a wardrobe detail, a physical feature that changes between shots of the
same continuous moment), *spatial continuity* (geography that
contradicts itself — a door on the wrong wall, a box that vanishes without
the story compressing time), or *temporal continuity* (an action that
implies a different amount of elapsed time than the cut claims). Naming
which of the three a finding is decides what fixing it would actually
involve.

## Transitions are a directorial choice

How one shot ends and the next begins is not a software default to accept
unexamined. A hard cut claims no time was skipped worth marking. A dissolve
claims a passage of time or a connection between two moments. A held frame
before the next shot begins claims the moment is still resolving. Choosing
between them is the same kind of directorial decision as picking a shot's
tactic in week 8 — it has to be made on purpose, not left to whatever the
edit tool inserts by default.

## The repair discipline: a real assembly and continuity log

This actual five-shot assembly of "Before It Stops" runs **31.23 seconds**:
kitchen wide → caller-screen insert → held reaction close-up → reach and
hover → acceptance and listening. The owner supplied the Jimeng footage;
the phone insert was generated with Sora 2 and accepted by the owner.
The reach-and-hover shot uses Week 7's Jimeng B candidate for this assembly.

- **Finding 1 — prop-state continuity: fix.** The reach shot originally
  begins with a dark phone after earlier shots establish a lit incoming
  call. Trim its first **1.3 seconds**, preserving the reach and hover.
  The new cut-entry frame was checked and shows the phone lit. This repairs
  the named mismatch without regenerating the performance.
- **Finding 2 — lighting variance: accept for this assembly.** The reaction
  close-up is warmer than the reach and acceptance shots. Identity, sweater
  and eyeline remain broadly consistent; retain the performance for this
  rough assembly rather than regenerate for the lighting shift alone.
- **Finding 3 — screen interface: accept for this assembly, record for the
  final film.** The insert has a dark incoming-call interface while the
  wider shots show a bright blue-white screen. The call remains active and
  the insert identifies Theo, so the edit retains the supplied footage.
  Matching the screen design remains a possible final-film repair.

Hard cuts keep the sequence in one continuous moment; a final half-second
fade ends the scene. The log records the decisions and their limits.

**Review limits:** findings come from actual sampled frames and cut-entry
checks, not an unpaused cold-watch or an independent group review. Original
audio is retained, but listening, exact "Hey" delivery and sound continuity
have not been verified. These are teaching materials, not proof that an
individual learner has completed the workshop or final assessment.

## Misconception contrast

| Reacting to a vague impression | Logging a precise finding |
|---|---|
| "Something feels off about this cut" | "The reach shot starts with a dark phone after the call is already lit" |
| Regenerating several shots to be safe | Regenerating only the shot the log names |
| Every variance treated as a problem | Distinguishing what has to match from what the eye forgives |
| Transitions left as the tool's default | Transitions chosen for what they claim about time and connection |

## Guided judgement: fix or accept?

Watching a cut cold, you notice the phone's screen brightness reads
slightly differently between the caller-screen insert (shot 2) and the reaction
close-up that follows (shot 3), both of the same continuous moment. Decide: fix, or
accept — and why.

<details>
<summary>Compare your diagnosis</summary>

Screen brightness on a prop, read across two shots of the same continuous
moment, is closer to lighting variance than to a geography or wardrobe
break — nothing about the story or the space contradicts itself, and nobody
watching uncued is likely to register a shift in how bright a phone screen
reads for half a second. This sits inside what the eye forgives: accept it,
and reserve the regeneration for a finding that actually changes what the
audience understands about the space or the moment.

</details>

For the workshop, apply the same test to your own cut: for every finding,
ask whether it's a geography, wardrobe, or temporal contradiction, or a
variance small enough to forgive — and let that answer decide fix or
accept.
