The last week turns the critique skill from week 9 back onto your own film,
and asks the question this whole course has been building toward: which
choices in the finished work are yours, and which are limitations you
learned to work around. Screening happens today; the final cut is due
eleven days later, and that gap is the revision period, not slack — the
critique you get in today's workshop is what it's for. The lecture teaches
and models the method on "Before It Stops"; your own near-final film screens
in a workshop group of four or five, followed by critique and defence.

## What you'll be able to do

- Run a last continuity and quality pass across the whole film, checked
  against your locked bible — Assignment 1's, or your pivot plan's if
  you've since switched stories — not against a fresh impression.
- Name and defend three directorial decisions in your own film against a
  specific alternative, and tell a directorial choice from a tool
  limitation you routed around.
- Choose the one note from today's critique worth acting on in the
  revision period, and leave the rest.

The lecture runs 90 minutes: recap and outcomes 10, technique and worked
defence 45, comparison 15, judgement practice 15, workshop briefing 5.

## How the lecture prepares you for the workshop

The 45-minute teaching block models a final quality check, specific critique,
decision defence and choice-versus-limitation using excerpts from "Before It
Stops." These short teaching screenings illustrate the method; the full
round of student films takes place in the workshop.

In the 15-minute comparison, contrast "it just felt right" with a defence
that names the rejected alternative and explains what the retained choice
does. In the 15-minute judgement practice, propose one specific alternative
for the example and decide whether to repair or retain the challenged choice.
The final five minutes explain the group screening roles and how to choose
one feedback note for the revision period.

## A last pass, checked against the bible

Before screening anything, the film gets one more continuity and quality
pass — but checked against your locked bible (Assignment 1's, or your
pivot plan's if you've since switched stories), the same reference week 9's
continuity log checked shots against, not against whatever feels right
about the film today. A late pass driven by fresh
impression is exactly the drift week 9 existed to catch; the bible is
still the fixed point, all the way to the end.

## The screening-and-critique ritual, formalized

Every major directing program researched for this course — AFI's dailies
review, NYU's weekly Master Class where all directing faculty screen every
student's cuts together, NFTS's dedicated rushes cinemas — shares one
recurring structural element: work in progress is screened to a group on a
fixed cadence and critiqued there, not simply marked at the end in
isolation. This week borrows that ritual directly, tightened into an
interview format close to AFI's Comprehensive Review, where a fellow
presents their own workbook and answers for it. In the workshop, your film
screens cold to your small group, then you defend specific decisions on the
spot. Groups run in parallel so every director receives time for feedback.

## Critique means naming a decision, not a feeling

*"I'd have cut to her reaction sooner"* is a critique — it names a specific
alternative, checkable against the choice actually made. *"It felt slow"*
isn't — it has nowhere to land, the same shapeless complaint week 9 ruled
out for a continuity finding and week 7 ruled out for a performance note.
Defending a decision means answering the specific alternative raised, not
restating that the choice was intentional. If a critique names an
alternative you hadn't considered, that's useful information regardless of
whether you act on it — the point of today isn't to win the exchange, it's
to find out which decisions actually hold up under a specific challenge.

## A worked defence

This is an authored worked defence of the format, not a report of an
actual screening.

Screening "Before It Stops," a critic proposes: *"I'd have shown Theo's
photo somewhere, so the audience has a face to attach to the call."*
Defence: that shot was drafted and cut in week 6, because the reach-and-
stop insert already argues the estrangement — adding Theo's face would
answer a question the film is deliberately leaving open, not fill a gap.
A second note: *"I'd start the music under the first ring."*
Defence: the actual Week 11 cue enters at 17.6 seconds, near the reach;
that placement puts its claim on attempted contact rather than announcing
the outcome at the interruption. This defends the produced placement against
a proposed alternative, not a claim that an earlier mix was repaired.
A third note: *"why does the call go unanswered for as
long as it does before she reaches?"* A possible editorial defence is to
retain a pause so the interruption of her final packing task registers
before contact. But Week 7's Jimeng B still delayed an intended immediate
reach. Name that generation mismatch honestly; retaining it in an assembly
does not prove the generated timing followed the note. Decide whether the
pause serves the cut before defending it as an editorial choice.

Three notes, three decisions defended against the specific alternative
raised — these are practice challenges to recorded choices, and none of
them answered with "it just felt right."

## Authorship: choice versus limitation

Not every decision in the finished film was made on purpose. Some were
routed around a limitation — a reference that stayed pending longer than
planned, a model that wouldn't follow a note no matter how it was
rewritten, a take that had to be accepted because no better one arrived in
time. Both are real facts about how the film was made. Only the first kind
is a choice you'd defend as the right call; the second is worth naming
honestly as a constraint worked within, not dressed up as an artistic
decision it wasn't.

## Misconception contrast

| Critique as opinion exchange | Critique as directing skill turned inward |
|---|---|
| "It felt slow" — nowhere to land | "I'd have cut to her reaction sooner" — a specific alternative |
| Defending everything as intentional | Naming what was a choice and what was a workaround |
| The revision period as time to polish everything raised | Picking the one note worth acting on, leaving the rest |
| A screening judged on how finished it looks | A screening judged on whether the decisions hold up defended aloud |

## Guided judgement: choice, or limitation?

A director explains that the phone case in the final shot doesn't match
the case shown in the earlier insert. Before checking, decide: is this
more likely a directorial choice or a limitation routed around, and what
would you ask to find out?

A visible prop mismatch between two shots of the same object is far more
likely a continuity failure than a deliberate choice — nothing about
"Before It Stops"'s premise calls for the phone to visibly change between
shots. The right question isn't "why does this serve the story," which
assumes it was a choice; it's "was this caught in week 9's continuity pass,
and if so, why wasn't it fixed" — which surfaces whether it's an accepted,
defensible trade-off (the eye-forgives category from week 9) or something
that slipped through and should be named honestly as a limitation, not
argued for as intentional.

For the workshop, apply the same test before defending your own decisions:
for each one, can you name the choice you made on purpose, or is it
actually a constraint you're describing as if it were a choice?

## What actually transfers

The loop this whole course has practised — direct, generate, evaluate,
redirect — doesn't belong to this semester's specific tools. It's the same
loop week 1 opened with, applied to a face, a voice, a cut, a schedule, and
now a whole finished film defended in front of a room. The tools generating
the images and sound will change; the discipline of naming exactly what you
want, reading exactly what you got, and directing the difference does not.

## What you take to the workshop

Bring your near-final cut and your director's statement so far.

You leave with a near-final cut that's been defended out loud, and one
specific critique note you intend to act on before the final deadline —
spend the revision period on the note that actually changes something, not
on polish nobody named.
