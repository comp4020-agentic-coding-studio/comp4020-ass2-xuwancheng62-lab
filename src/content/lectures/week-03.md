A logline and a premise say what happens. A screenplay has to say it in a
form specific enough that a director can add a note to it — and last
week's premise for "Before It Stops" ends on a moment neither of those
forms can carry: *on the last ring before the call would go to voicemail,
she finally accepts and says "Hey," then listens.* That's a playable
action, not a sentence of prose. This week is about the form that holds
it, and the note that tells someone how to play it.

## What you'll be able to do after this lecture

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

- write one scene in standard screenplay form — slugline, action, dialogue
  — without describing a feeling directly in either column
- given a moment that needs directing, write an intent note that names the
  concrete antecedent circumstance behind it, not an adjective describing
  the desired mood
- tell an antecedent-circumstance note apart from an adjective note, in
  someone else's writing as well as your own

## Form exists so you don't have to say how it feels

Screenplay form has three moving parts, and each one has a job:

- a **slugline** (INT./EXT., location, time) fixes where and when, so
  nothing later has to re-establish it
- an **action line** writes what a camera can actually see — a body doing
  something, an object changing state — never a feeling, an intention, or
  a metaphor
- **dialogue** is what a character is willing to say out loud, which is
  usually not what they mean

The blank space around the page matters as much as the words on it: form
that's dense with description is form that's already decided how the
moment should be played, leaving nothing for a note — or a director — to
add.

## Weston's "moment before": the antecedent circumstance

An intent note in the margin is where a director tells a performer (or a
model) how a line or action should land. The single most common way notes
fail is the same failure week 1 named for a wish: they name a *feeling*
instead of something playable. Judith Weston's directing technique for
this — "the moment before" — is to invent the
concrete, playable circumstance that happened immediately *before* the
line, rather than reaching for an adjective to describe the line itself.

Two ways to note the same line:

- **Adjective note**: "Say it nervously." This names a mood and asks the
  performer to manufacture it directly — there's nothing to actually *do*,
  only a feeling to perform from the outside.
- **Antecedent-circumstance note**: "You've just watched your own hand
  move before you decided to let it." This names a concrete event that
  happened one second earlier, in-world, that a performer can play the
  consequence of. The nervousness, if it shows up at all, is a byproduct
  of that circumstance — not the instruction itself.

A note that names an antecedent circumstance is playable the same way
week 1's playable action was: it's one specific thing that just happened,
not a description of how the result should look.

## From premise to a two-page scene: the writing process

Before this week's worked example arrives already finished, here's the
process that builds one — the same sequence this week's workshop asks you
to run on your own film, using your own Week 2 premise:

premise → decisive scene → scene change → five observable beats →
screenplay form → director's note

**Premise.** Week 2 already gives you a want, an obstacle, and what
happens if the want fails. For "Before It Stops": Nadia wants to answer
Theo's call; years of estrangement make her hold herself back; if she
fails, this call goes unanswered.

**Decisive scene.** A premise can cover more ground than two pages will
hold. Pick the one scene where the want and the obstacle actually meet —
not a scene that explains the situation, one where something happens
because of it. For "Before It Stops," that's the kitchen in her late
mother's apartment, while the phone rings itself out.

**Scene change.** Name the scene's beginning and end before writing a
single line of action. At the beginning, Nadia maintains the silence. At
the end, Nadia answers. The visible change is her hand moving from
restraint to accepting the call. If you can't name a beginning and an end
that differ, you don't have a scene yet — you have a setting.

**Five observable beats.** Break the distance between beginning and end
into beats an audience can actually see or hear — not what a character
privately feels or decides, which nobody in a cinema has access to. For
"Before It Stops":

1. Theo calls.
2. Nadia sees his name but does not answer.
3. The ringing continues and the opportunity narrows.
4. Her hand moves toward the phone and stops.
5. On the final ring, she accepts the call.

A beat is something the audience can see or hear — a phone lighting up, a
hand moving, a ring continuing. It is not a shot, a camera move, a cut, or
a choice about coverage: those decisions belong to week 4, once a scene
like this one already exists to break down.

**Screenplay form.** Once the five beats are fixed, each one becomes a
line of screenplay form, in order — a slugline places it, action lines
show what's visible, and dialogue only appears where a beat actually needs
a line spoken out loud:

```
INT. MOTHER'S APARTMENT - KITCHEN - NIGHT

Nadia sits alone at the table. Her phone lights up: THEO CALLING.

She looks at the screen. She does not move.

The phone keeps ringing.

Her hand drifts toward it. Stops.

On the last ring, her hand closes over the phone. She answers.

NADIA
Hey.
```

Beats one through four need no dialogue at all — the action line already
shows everything the audience needs. Beat five is the one place a line
earns its place: it's the first thing she's said in the scene, and it's
who she's saying it to that matters, not what she says.

**Director's note.** The screenplay above is directable but not yet
directed — the same distinction week 1 drew between a wish and a note.
The section above already gave you the technique for writing that note;
the demonstration below shows it applied to this exact scene.

## The real demonstration

The scene this week's example writes is the beat the premise ended on:
last ring before voicemail, and the moment just after.

Read the [full scene](/resources/week-03/scene.txt) now, before reading
any further here. As you read, form your own answer to two questions:
what is Nadia holding back during the held look while the phone rings,
and what's happening on the line "Hey" when she finally picks up? Decide
before opening either note below.

<details>
<summary>The note on the held look, while the phone rings</summary>

She has just taped the last box from her mother's kitchen shut, seconds
before the phone lit up with Theo's name. That's the antecedent
circumstance — not a mood word like "sad" or "reluctant" attached to the
look itself, and not a result like "she is refusing to let herself want
to answer," which describes what the look should accomplish rather than
what happened a moment before it. Whatever the look actually
communicates is a byproduct of what she's just finished doing, not an
instruction for how to hold it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>The note on the line "Hey," once she picks up</summary>

Her hand has already pressed to accept the call before she's registered
deciding to — Theo can hear her now. This is the antecedent-circumstance
move in miniature: the note doesn't say "say it too fast" or "say it
nervously." It names what just happened a half-second earlier (the hand
moved ahead of the decision that was supposed to precede it) and leaves
the line's actual delivery — however that lands — as the consequence of
that circumstance, not a result stated in the note itself.

</details>

Now compare your own reading to both notes. However close your prediction
came, notice what the notes *don't* do: neither one tells you how the
moment should look or sound. That judgement — what a held look or a
rushed "Hey" would actually communicate — is yours to make from the
circumstance, the same way it would be a performer's or a model's to make
on set.

Once you've made that comparison, you can see both notes back in place in
the margin of the scene itself: the
[annotated scene](/resources/week-03/scene-annotated.txt) shows exactly
where each one attaches.

## A wish for a scene, not just a line

Week 1 taught the wish/note distinction for a single directed take. The
same failure shows up at the scene level, at larger scale:

| Wish for the scene | Note for the scene |
|---|---|
| "Make the ending feel inevitable." | "Tomorrow the movers empty this kitchen for good — tonight's the last night it's hers to sit in." |

The wish names an effect on the audience and stops there. The note names
a concrete fact that's already true before the scene starts — not a
description of what Nadia believes or how that belief changes over the
scene, which just relocates the same result-first failure from an
adjective into a sentence about her interior state. What the note gives
instead is something that has already happened, off the page, that the
whole scene now plays out under. Whether that produces stillness,
restlessness, or something else entirely is left open — the same way an
antecedent-circumstance note does for a single line.

## Guided judgement: what's the antecedent circumstance?

A student's scene has a character slam a door and say one clipped line.
Their intent note reads: *"Play it furious."* Before rewriting it, name
what's wrong: it's an adjective note, describing the desired result
rather than what happened a moment before it. A fix has to invent a
concrete, playable circumstance — for example: *"She's just heard, through
the door, the one thing she asked him never to repeat."* That's something
a performer can play the consequence of; "furious" is something they can
only perform from the outside.

## Synthesis

Screenplay form writes what a camera can see and what a character says out
loud — never a feeling directly. An intent note in the margin does the
same job at the level of a single moment: it names the antecedent
circumstance that produced the line or action, not an adjective describing
how it should land. Both are the same discipline week 1 named — a wish
gives a performer nothing to do; a note, whether it's a playable action or
an antecedent circumstance, gives them something concrete to play. This
week's scene and its intent note travel forward as-is: next week you break
this exact scene into shots.
