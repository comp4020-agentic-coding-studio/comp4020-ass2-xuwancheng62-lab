A director doesn't make the shot. A director says what a shot is for,
decides whether it did that, and tells someone — a cinematographer, an
actor, a model — exactly what to change. That loop is the entire craft
this course practices: **direct → generate → evaluate → redirect**. It's
the same loop whether the person on the other end is a human actor or a
model: what changes with an AI cast and crew is turnaround time and the
cost of being wrong; what doesn't change is judgement, taste, and the
discipline of saying precisely what's wrong.

This course uses one running story to make every week's technique
concrete: **"Before It Stops"** — on the final night before her late
mother's apartment is cleared, a woman receives a call from the brother
she hasn't spoken to in years, and must decide whether to answer before
the ringing stops. It's a worked teaching example, not a project you're
being asked to adopt — what you actually direct, later in the course, is
your own choice.

## What you'll be able to do after this lecture

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

- tell a director's note apart from a wish, and rewrite a wish as a note
- given a scene's stated objective and the interpretation it's meant to
  produce, name one playable, observable action that would realise it
- judge a take against the objective and interpretation it was meant to
  produce — not against whether it's generically well made — and diagnose
  the specific gap when it misses
- tell a genuine redirect (adjusting the playable action, timing,
  emphasis, or degree) apart from a note that quietly swaps in a different
  objective

## The chain: intention, objective, action, result, interpretation

Every note this course asks you to write comes from the same chain, and
skipping a link in it is why notes fail.

- **Intention** is what you, the director, want this moment to mean.
- An **objective** turns that intention into something the character is
  actively trying to do or get, inside the scene — not a mood, a want.
- A **playable action** is the one specific, observable thing a performer
  (or a model) can actually execute to pursue that objective: a look, a
  pause, a shift in posture, a change of pace.
- The **result** is whatever gets generated when that action is executed.
- The **interpretation** is what an audience — someone who wasn't told any
  of the above — reads into the result.

The chain only works end to end. An intention that never becomes an
objective stays an abstraction nobody can act on. An objective that never
becomes a playable action is still just a want in your head. And a
playable action that doesn't produce the intended interpretation hasn't
failed randomly — it's failed at one identifiable link, which is exactly
what you diagnose when you evaluate a take.

Take this week's scene. A woman sits alone at a kitchen table at night —
her late mother's apartment, cleared out around her, the last night before
it's gone for good. Her phone, beside her on the table, vibrates once: her
brother, calling.

- **Objective**: she wants to answer but consciously prevents herself from
  doing so.
- **Intended interpretation**: an audience should read her stillness as
  restraint — a difficult choice being made in real time — not as
  indifference, boredom, or an absent-minded pause.
- **Playable action**: something like "reach toward the phone, stop your
  fingertips just short of it, hold for a full second, then withdraw" is
  playable — it's one instruction a performer or a model can actually
  execute. "Show she wants to answer but won't" is not; it just restates
  the objective in different words and gives nobody anything to do.

Everything this week's workshop asks of you — diagnosing a provided Take A
against a fixed objective, writing Note B as a genuine redirect — is
practising this same translation: the objective and the intended
interpretation stay fixed, and your job is finding the playable action
that actually produces them.

<summary>Reveal Note B and why it is a genuine redirect</summary>

**Note B:** At the vibration, inhale and snap the eyes to the phone; reach

second with tense fingers and a tightened jaw; then deliberately withdraw
the hand into the lap while keeping the eyes fixed on the phone, ending on
a controlled exhale.

**Why this is a genuine redirect:** Note B doesn't change what the
character wants or what the audience should understand. It targets the
diagnosed gap by changing only observable behaviour — onset speed,
stopping distance, duration of the hover, muscular tension, eye line, and
the endpoint of the withdrawal — turning an ambiguous slow reach into a
legible act of self-restraint while preserving the original objective.

Check the differences you observed against the note. Specificity matters
because it targets a diagnosed gap while preserving the objective.

## A note, not a wish

A **wish** names a feeling you want more of. A **note** names one
specific, observable action — a way of realising an objective — and
roughly when and how much. Wishes fail for a chain reason, not just a
vagueness reason: they skip straight from intention to result, naming
neither an objective nor a playable action, so there's nothing for a
performer or a model to actually do.

| Wish | Note |
|---|---|
| "Make it more emotional" | "Look up half a second before you say it" |
| "It doesn't feel right" | "Hold the pause one beat longer before the line" |

Both notes on the right name a playable action and roughly when it
happens. Neither invents a new objective — they're translations of an
existing one into something executable.

## Guided judgement: redirect, or a different note?

Say a take on this same scene came back reading as boredom, not restraint
— the wrong interpretation for the stated objective. Which of these two
notes is a genuine redirect, and which one has quietly swapped in a
different objective?

1. "Hold the hand a half-second closer to the phone, and let her exhale
   shake slightly before she pulls back."
2. "Make her seem like she's already decided the call doesn't matter."

The first is a redirect: same objective, same intended interpretation, an
adjustment to timing plus a small added detail — a genuinely playable next
step. The second isn't a redirect at all; it directs toward a different
character, one who's actually stopped caring whether the call is
answered. Confusing the two is the most common failure in a redirect:
closing a gap by inventing a new scene instead of correcting the one you
already committed to.

## Synthesis

The chain — intention, objective, playable action, result, interpretation
— is the tool for both writing a note and diagnosing why a take missed.
Every later week in this course adds a new layer onto this same chain: how
an objective survives a shot list, how a moment-before produces the
objective in the first place, how a cut argues for one interpretation over
another. None of that replaces this week's judgement; it depends on it.

"Before It Stops" stays this course's worked example throughout — useful
because it's small enough to think through completely, not because it's
the film you're required to make.
