Every hour spent planning here is an hour not spent waiting on a queue for a
shot that was never going to be in the film. This week is the last one before
generation starts, and the discipline is to spend it, not skip it.

## What you'll be able to do

- Expand a scene-level shot list and storyboard into a complete, sequenced
  schedule for the whole short, not just its most dramatic scene.
- State which bible reference a shot's keyframe(s) draw on, and which
  keyframe(s) a generated shot depends on in turn — ordering shots so
  nothing is scheduled before what it needs already exists.
- Present a shot list and storyboard aloud, defend a shot you cut, and judge
  the package complete against a stated bar.
- Recognize that a take matching both of a shot's keyframes isn't
  automatically a finished shot — the movement between them still has to be
  directed and evaluated.

The lecture runs 90 minutes: recap 5, outcomes 5, technique and worked
schedule 45, comparison 15, judgement practice 15, synthesis 5.

## A shot list is a schedule, not a wish list

Week 4 built a shot list for one dramatic beat and asked what each shot
argues. This week asks a second question: in what order does each shot need
to be generated, and what has to be locked before that generation is
worth attempting? A wish list orders shots by how exciting they are to
imagine. A schedule orders them by dependency: the screenplay locked in
week 3, the bible locked in week 5, and — for shots involving a specific
character or location — the exact reference version that shot needs.

A shot that depends on an unresolved reference isn't ready to generate.
Scheduling it anyway wastes the queue time this week exists to protect.
Naming the dependency is the planning work; waiting for the decision is not
a delay this week causes, it's a delay this week catches before it costs
you a generation.

## Bible, keyframes, generated shots: three different jobs

"A shot depends on a reference" is true but not specific enough to plan
against once generation actually starts. Three different artefacts sit
between the bible and the finished shot, and each one fixes a different
kind of decision:

- **The bible** fixes identity: a character's or location's appearance,
  costume, props, spatial relationships, lighting, and visual style —
  the facts that must hold across every shot that uses them.
- **A keyframe** translates those fixed facts into one specific still for
  one specific shot: its framing, the pose, the expression, where a prop
  sits in frame — and, for a shot built as a single continuous take rather
  than a cut, both the state it starts in and the state it ends in.
- **The generated shot** takes the keyframe(s) and adds what a still image
  can't show: performance, action, timing, and camera movement.

This is the same fixed-identity-versus-changing-state split week 5 taught
for a bible entry, one level further down. Two keyframes for the *same*
shot can — and, for a continuous shot, must — differ from each other in
pose, prop position, and framing, while both still have to match the same
fixed bible identity. A keyframe changing Nadia's face or costume between
its start and end state is a continuity break; a keyframe changing where
her hand is relative to the phone is the entire point of having two of
them.

Matching both keyframes is necessary, not sufficient. A generated take can
land exactly on its starting still and exactly on its ending still and
still fail as a shot, because the movement connecting them — how the
performance is timed, what the camera does while it happens — is a
separate directorial decision that has to be directed and evaluated on its
own terms, not assumed from the keyframes alone. Week 7 develops directing
that movement in depth; this week's job is only to see that it's a
distinct step, not a consequence of getting the keyframes right.

Which keyframes a tool actually needs is tool-dependent, and this course
doesn't prescribe it in advance. Where a generation tool accepts both a
starting and an ending reference image, supply both. Where a tool accepts
only a single starting image, the starting keyframe becomes that
generation's input, and the ending keyframe becomes what you evaluate the
resulting take against — a target for judging the take, not a second input
every tool consumes. Check what a given tool actually supports before
assuming either shape.

## The real demonstration: the whole short as a schedule

Week 2's premise estimated three segments for "Before It Stops": clearing
the kitchen and settling at the table (0:00–1:25), the phone ringing while
she reaches, stops, and withdraws (1:25–1:55), and accepting on the final
ring (1:55–2:10). Week 4 shot-listed only the closing five shots. Expanding
to the whole short means shot-listing the first segment too, then
sequencing everything as one schedule:

| # | Shot | Keyframe(s) | Bible reference(s) the keyframe(s) need | What the generated shot directs beyond the keyframe(s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Wide — she tapes the last box shut among the half-packed kitchen, sets it on the stack by the door | One: wide framing on the sealed box and the stack by the door | Kitchen reference | The taping and setting-down action, timed |
| 2 | Medium — she crosses to the table and sits, phone resting nearby | One: medium framing, seated at the table, phone on the table | Kitchen + Nadia references | The crossing and sitting motion leading into the held frame |
| 3 | Wide — the figure at the kitchen table, phone lighting up beside her | One: wide framing, phone lit | Kitchen + Nadia + phone references | The phone's light-up timing against her stillness |
| 4 | Insert — the phone screen reads THEO CALLING | One: tight insert on the lit screen | Phone reference | Screen/ring timing, the held camera |
| 5 | Close-up — her face, still | One: close framing, restrained expression | Nadia reference | Held stillness, breath, minimal motion |
| 6 | Insert — her fingers reach toward the phone and stop short of it | One: hand mid-reach, the air gap held | Nadia + phone references | The reach's speed and the stop, timed |
| 7 | Close-up, continuous — camera tilts up as she accepts on the final ring, lifts the phone to her ear, and says "Hey" | Two: **start** — low framing on her hand and the phone, still on the table; **end** — the phone at her ear as she says "Hey" | start: Nadia + kitchen + phone references; end: Nadia + phone references | The accept-and-lift performance, its timing, and the camera's upward tilt connecting the two keyframes without a cut |

Shots 3–7 are week 4's closing five, unchanged, renumbered into their place
in the whole sequence. Shots 1–2 shot-list the opening segment that week 2
only described in prose. Shot 7's keyframe pair is an authored planning
example illustrating the dependency chain, the same status as the rest of
this schedule — not a report of an actual generation.

Read the "Bible reference(s)" column against week 5's bible as it actually
stands today, not as an unresolved wish list. Week 5 now embeds an image
for each of these: [Nadia's appearance
reference](/resources/week-05/images/nadia-week1-reference-v1.png), [the
kitchen's layout and lighting
reference](/resources/week-05/images/kitchen-week1-reference-v1.png), and
the phone's appearance (carried over from week 4's [phone
reference](/resources/week-04/images/bis-02-phone-week1-v1.png)). One
dependency is still genuinely open — the kitchen reference's full geometry
beyond what's pictured is inferred, and its production approval is still
pending. That's the schedule doing its job either way: before this pass it
would have shown you six shots waiting on the same handful of undecided
references; now it shows you one shot (1) still waiting on one open
decision, and the rest ready to prepare.

Narrative order and preparation order are not the same list. Shot 1 comes
first on screen, but its keyframe needs the kitchen at a level of finished
detail — full room geometry — that's still only inferred and awaiting
approval. Shot 6's keyframe needs only Nadia and the phone, both already
resolved, so it can be prepared before shot 1 is, even though it happens
much later in the story. Prepare keyframes in the order their dependencies
actually clear, not in narrative order.

## Defending a cut: the AFI Comprehensive Review

Program research on AFI's Conservatory documents a **Comprehensive Review**:
fellows present their own workbook and shot decisions aloud and answer for
them, rather than submitting a document that's only ever read silently. This
week's workshop borrows that format directly: you don't just hand over a
shot list, you say it aloud and defend at least one cut.

Here is a worked cut. An earlier draft of this schedule included an insert
of a photograph of young Nadia and Theo, taped to the fridge, meant to
explain the estrangement visually. Cut, because week 4's rule already
applies here: no two shots may argue the same thing. The reach-and-stop
insert (shot 6) already tells the audience she is holding herself back from
someone who matters to her — a photograph restates that same argument in a
second shot instead of adding a new one. The schedule is shorter for
dropping it, not weaker.

## Misconception contrast

| Treating the storyboard as decoration | Treating it as the production schedule |
|---|---|
| Drawn after the scene is imagined, to illustrate it | Built before any generation, to decide what order things happen in |
| A shot is "in" because it would look good | A shot is in because it argues something no other shot argues |
| Gaps get noticed once something is missing on set | Gaps get noticed by checking every shot's dependency before scheduling it |
| More shots reads as more complete | Complete means no shot without a stated purpose, in dependency order |
| Two keyframes that match the bible is a finished shot | The movement between them still has to be directed and evaluated on its own terms |

## Guided judgement: what's wrong with this schedule?

A student's draft schedule lists, in this order: (1) an insert of the
phone ringing, generated first because "it's the easiest shot to try"; (2)
a wide of the kitchen, scheduled after, because the reference for it "will
probably be ready by then." Identify the ordering problem before opening
the diagnosis.

<details>
<summary>Compare your diagnosis</summary>

Scheduling by ease or optimism is a wish list's logic, not a schedule's.
The insert of the phone depends on the phone prop reference exactly as
much as any other shot touching it — being quick to attempt doesn't make it
ready. The wide shot is scheduled on a reference that "will probably be
ready," which is a guess about the future, not a locked dependency. Both
shots need their references resolved and recorded before they're
scheduled, in whichever order the references actually land — not the order
that feels most convenient to attempt.

</details>

For the workshop, apply the same test to your own schedule: for every
shot, name its keyframe(s), name what each one depends on, and don't
schedule anything ahead of a dependency that isn't locked yet.

## What you take to the workshop

Bring your complete shot list and storyboard — the whole short, sequenced
from the first frame to the last, not one scene of it. The [shot list and
storyboard template](/resources/week-04/shot-list-template.html) from week
4 extends to as many rows as your full sequence needs. Be ready to name
each shot's keyframe(s) and which bible reference each one needs, not just
the schedule's order.

You leave with a schedule you can present aloud and defend, and one shot
you can point to and explain why it didn't earn its place. Keep it with your
locked bible for Assignment 1.
