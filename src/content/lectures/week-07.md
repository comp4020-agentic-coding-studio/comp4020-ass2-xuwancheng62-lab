An anchor frame is a locked starting point, generated straight from the
bible so it matches everything already decided. What happens next is where
directing actually starts: not what's in the frame, but what changes inside
it over the next few seconds.

## What you'll be able to do

- Generate an anchor image from a locked bible reference, rather than a
  fresh description that risks drifting from it.
- Write a "moment before" for a beat — the concrete circumstance just
  before it — and use it to direct a motion take's timing, not just its
  content.
- Name what changed between two motion takes in the language of
  performance (timing, a look, a gesture), not prompt syntax.

The lecture runs 90 minutes: recap 5, outcomes 5, technique and worked
takes 45, comparison 15, judgement practice 15, synthesis 5.

## The anchor frame is a locked starting point, not a fresh guess

Generate the anchor image straight from the character and location bible
entries in your locked pre-production package — Assignment 1's, or your
pivot plan's if you've since switched stories — the same references a
shot's storyboard frame already points to — rather than writing a new
description of the scene from memory. A fresh description drifts; a generation built from the
locked reference has something to be checked against. The anchor is
generated from whichever bible reference(s) that shot needs; where one is
still pending — today, only the kitchen's full production approval —
generating an anchor tied to it still has to wait.

Once the anchor exists, directing hasn't started yet — it's the same
discipline as picking a starting mark for an actor before rehearsing the
scene. What you actually direct is what changes across the next few
seconds of motion: a held breath, a delayed reach, a look that arrives a
beat late.

## The moment before: a concrete antecedent, not a mood word

Judith Weston's technique for directing a beat is the **moment before**:
invent the specific, playable circumstance that happened immediately
before the scripted action, rather than relying on a script's own
parenthetical — "(pause)," "(beat)" — which describes a gap, not something
an actor or a model can play. A moment-before note answers a concrete
question: what just happened, a second ago, that makes this beat happen
the way it does?

For the closing beat of "Before It Stops," the written action is: on the
final ring, she accepts the call, lifts the phone to her ear, and says
"Hey." That line names the outcome. It doesn't yet direct how the lift
happens. For this demonstration, the shared moment-before is that she has just
finished packing her mother's kitchen and her estranged brother's call has
interrupted that final task. It supports the resistance to contact without
adding earlier missed calls to the story. Direct the playable difference
through when her right hand begins moving, keeping the outcome fixed.

## Misconception contrast

| Directing the frame's contents | Directing the moment before |
|---|---|
| "Make the kitchen feel colder" — aimed at the room | "She has just finished packing; delay the reach before stopping short" — aimed at the action |
| A note with nowhere to land in the performance | A note the timing of the reach can visibly play |
| Judged by whether the shot looks good | Judged by whether the antecedent is legible in the take |
| Two takes differing in lighting or camera speed for no stated reason | Two takes differing in exactly the directed beat, and nothing else |

## Guided judgement: what's the note actually directing?

A student's note reads: "Make her hesitation more dramatic." Identify what's
wrong with it as a moment-before, and propose a concrete antecedent that
would fix it.

<details>
<summary>Compare your diagnosis</summary>

"More dramatic" describes a mood, not a circumstance — it gives the take
nothing specific to play, the same failure as a script's bare "(beat)."
A working antecedent names a fact: *she sealed the last box from her
mother's kitchen only seconds before the phone lit up.* That circumstance
gives a performer, or a model directed toward performance, something
concrete to carry into the reach — a longer stillness motivated by the
specific task just interrupted, not a vaguer instruction to seem more
torn.

</details>

For the workshop, apply the same test to your own note before generating:
does it name a circumstance, or a mood? Only a circumstance is directable.

## What you take to the workshop

Bring your approved shot list and bible from Assignment 1, and pick one
shot to animate. Write a moment-before for its central beat before you
generate anything.

You leave with an anchor image and two takes that differ by exactly one
directed choice, timed against a concrete antecedent — the same discipline
from week 1, now with motion attached.
