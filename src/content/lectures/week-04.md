A scene on the page is one thing happening. A scene on screen is a sequence
of decisions about what the audience is allowed to see, and when. Last
week's scene for "Before It Stops" ends with Nadia accepting the call on the
final ring and saying "Hey" — five observable beats, written in screenplay
form, with nothing yet said about how a camera would see any of them. This
week turns that page into a shot sequence.

## What you'll be able to do after this lecture

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

- name each core shot type and what it's actually *for*, not just reach for
  variety
- build a shot list where every shot's purpose is stated in one clause, and
  no two shots in the list share a purpose
- tell a shot list built for coverage apart from one built as an argument —
  in someone else's list as well as your own

## The shot vocabulary — and what each one is for

Four shot types do almost all of the work in a small scene, and each earns
its place for a different reason:

- a **wide** shot establishes place, distance, or isolation — how much space
  is around a body, and how alone they are in it
- a **medium** shot reads body language and the relation between two
  people — posture, distance between them, who's turned toward whom
- a **close-up** shows a decision happening on a face — the moment where an
  internal choice becomes visible, even briefly
- an **insert** isolates one detail the story needs the audience to notice
  and nothing else — an object, a hand, a screen

*The diagrams through the rest of this lecture are teaching illustrations —
schematic staging references, not filmed footage from a real production
test.*

None of these is a default. Reaching for "wide, then medium, then close, on

decision until the edit, and it's exactly the failure mode this week's
misconception contrast, below, names directly.

## Composition, blocking and camera movement as one decision

Where the frame's edge falls says something about power, distance, or
intimacy before a single word is spoken — a body pushed to the frame's edge
reads differently from the same body centred. That's composition and angle
working as argument, not decoration.

Blocking (where a body moves) and camera movement (where the camera moves)
are one decision, not two. A character crossing a room while the camera
holds still says something different from the same crossing with the camera
tracking alongside her — the first isolates the room around her, the second
stays with her attention. Deciding one without the other leaves half the
shot undecided.

## Mamet's test: the shot as argument

David Mamet's directing technique for building a shot list is to treat each
shot as a piece of an argument, built through the *juxtaposition* of shots
rather than through performance, narration, or camera movement doing the
persuading. His own worked example: cutting from a face to a fridge's bare
interior doesn't need a line of dialogue to argue "hunger" — the meaning is
made entirely by what gets placed next to what.

That gives a concrete filter for every shot on the list, and it's the same
filter for the whole list, not a separate rule per shot: **what does the
character want in this moment, and will the audience see whether they get
it, from this shot next to this one?** A shot that doesn't answer that
question for a specific beat isn't earning a place on the list — it's
coverage, filmed so an editing decision can be postponed rather than made
now.

This is also why a shot list's purposes can't repeat. If two shots exist to
show the same thing, one of them isn't making an argument at all — it's a
safety copy of the other.

## The real demonstration: shot-listing the "Before It Stops" ending

Before seeing the worked list, try one beat yourself. Beat 4 from last
week's scene is: *her hand drifts toward the phone, then stops.* If you had
exactly one shot to make that beat's hesitation legible without a line of
dialogue, what would it be — and what would you cut to or from it to make
the meaning land? Decide before opening the shot list below.

<details>
<summary>The worked shot list for the closing beats</summary>

Five beats, five shots — one shot per beat, each filtered through what
Nadia wants and whether she's getting it:

1. **Wide — the kitchen, phone lighting up on the table.** *Purpose:
   establish how alone she is with this decision.* A close-up here would
   argue the phone matters before we've even placed her in the room; the
   wide argues isolation instead.
2. **Insert — the screen reading THEO CALLING.** *Purpose: give the
   audience the same single fact she has.* Cutting straight from the wide
   to this insert (skipping a shot of her face) keeps the audience learning
   the name at the same instant she does, not a beat ahead of her.
3. **Close-up — her face, still.** *Purpose: show her holding herself back,
   not deciding yet.* Juxtaposed against the insert before it, the cut
   argues "she's seen it and isn't moving" — the stillness only reads as
   restraint because it follows immediately after the name, not before it.
4. **Insert — her hand, drifting toward the phone, stopping.** *Purpose:
   make the hesitation itself the visible event.* This is the shot beat 4
   asked for above: an insert isolates the hand from the rest of her body,
   so the stopping is the only thing the frame can be about.
5. **Close-up — camera framed on the tabletop and the lower half of her
   face, phone in shot; her hand enters frame, closes over the phone, and
   lifts it toward her ear as the camera tilts up with it to settle on her
   face for the line.** *Purpose: land the decision and the line as one
   motion, not two.* This is one take, not a cut: the frame starts low, on
   the phone and her hand's approach; as her hand closes over it and brings
   it up, the camera tilts up in the same movement, arriving on her face
   just as she says "Hey." Because the reframe happens inside a single
   shot, there's no cut between the acceptance and the line for the
   audience to read as two separate choices — the camera's move *is* the
   argument that they're one decision.

No shot repeats another's purpose, and no shot is a wide/medium/close set
covering the same instant "for options" — each one exists because the cut
into it argues something the previous shot hadn't yet.

## Coverage instinct vs. argument instinct

| Coverage instinct | Argument instinct |
|---|---|
| Shoot a wide, a medium, and a close-up of the phone-ringing beat, so there are options in the edit | Choose the one shot whose cut produces the meaning this beat needs — an insert on the still hand, not three angles of the same stillness |

The coverage instinct isn't wrong because it's lazy — it's wrong because it
defers the actual directing decision (what does this beat mean, and which
shot proves it) to someone else, later, in an edit bay. The argument
instinct makes that decision now, on the list, before a single frame exists.

## Guided judgement: which pairing makes the argument?

A beat: a character is at the kitchen table, a housemate visible in the
background doing the dishes. The character reads a text message, then sets
the phone face-down on the table, fingers lingering on it a second before
withdrawing. The intention behind the beat is **concealment**: they don't
want the housemate to see what's on that screen — not simply "they finished
reading a text." Two candidate shot pairings:

1. A medium shot of them reading, with the housemate already visible
   working at the sink behind them, cut to a wide shot of the same kitchen
   as they set the phone down.
2. A close-up of their face reading, tight enough that the housemate drops
   out of frame, cut to an insert of the phone, screen down, their fingers
   still resting on it.

**Beat:** reading a text, setting it face-down, fingers lingering.
**Intention:** concealment from the housemate visible in the background.

The first pairing is coverage for *this* intention: the medium already
placed the housemate in the kitchen, so the wide's cut to the same room
tells the audience nothing new about the concealment — it repeats
information the medium already gave. The second is an argument: tightening
to a close-up removes the housemate from view the instant the concealment
becomes active, and the insert of the phone held face-down under lingering
fingers is the visible shape of "hiding something," not just "put the phone
down." Same beat, same two shots' worth of screen time; only the second
pairing's cut is doing work toward this specific intention.

This verdict is scoped to this beat's intention, not a rule about wide
shots. A wide isn't inherently meaningless — shot 1 of this week's own
demonstration is a wide, chosen specifically to argue isolation. What fails
here is that *this* wide, for *this* beat's concealment intention, adds
nothing the medium hasn't already shown; a wide built to argue something
the prior shot hadn't yet (scale, isolation, who else is in the room) could
easily be the argument instinct instead of the coverage one.

## Synthesis

A shot list isn't a coverage plan — it's a sequence of arguments, each one
made by what gets cut next to what, filtered through what the character
wants and whether the audience sees them get it. Wide, medium, close-up and
insert each earn their place for a specific reason, and a purpose stated
once per shot, never repeated, is how you check that every shot on the list
is still doing that work. This week's task turns last week's scene into
exactly that kind of list, plus a rough thumbnail per shot — the first-draft
storyboard that week 6 later expands into the full sequence. A blank,
printable [shot list and storyboard template](/resources/week-04/shot-list-template.html)
is available for the workshop.
