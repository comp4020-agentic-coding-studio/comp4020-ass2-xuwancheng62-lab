Before you can direct anything you need a story that survives being small.
A feature-length premise doesn't fit a semester or a two-to-four-minute
runtime; most of what goes wrong in short-form drama happens at the logline
stage, before a single shot exists. Short-form dramatic structure isn't a
plot — it's one want, one obstacle, one change. First test the want and
obstacle in a logline; then use the premise to test whether the change can
be shown within the film's limits.

## What you'll be able to do after this lecture

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

- tell a testable logline apart from a summary, and rewrite a summary as a
  test
- given any logline, name the protagonist's want *and* the concrete
  obstacle in the way — not a mood, an obstacle
- apply the question "so what happens if they don't get it?" to a logline,
  including your own, and tell whether it survives being asked
- test a premise against its runtime, characters, and location by naming
  observable actions and the change they produce
- explain why short-form drama favors an emotional hook and observable
  behaviour over lengthy dialogue or abstract internal monologue, and
  check a premise against that

## The logline as a claim, not a summary

A logline isn't a description of the film — it's a claim you can be held
to later, in week 6 and beyond, when the shot list and the screenplay both
have to agree with it. Start with two questions: what does the character
want, and what might prevent them from getting it? A logline compresses
that dramatic question into one sentence, and it only works if it names
two testable parts:

- **the objective** — what the protagonist actively wants, right now, not
  a feeling they're having
- **the obstacle** — what is concretely in the way of getting it, specific
  enough that removing it would resolve the story

Stakes follow from these two: they're what's lost if the obstacle wins.
A sentence that states an objective and stakes but never names a concrete
obstacle isn't finished yet, no matter how dramatic it sounds.

## Worked example: from decision to desire

Here is the **initial draft** for "Before It Stops":

> On the final night before her late mother's apartment is cleared, a woman
> must decide whether to answer a call from the brother she hasn't spoken
> to in years, before the ringing stops.

It names a situation and a deadline, but "must decide whether" does not
tell us which outcome she wants. She could want to answer, avoid him, or
end their relationship. If the premise is needed to supply the want, the
logline has not passed its own test yet.

The **revised reference logline** makes the conflict explicit:

> On the final night before her late mother's apartment is cleared, Nadia
> wants to answer her estranged brother's call but must overcome her own
> restraint after years of silence, or let this chance for contact pass
> when the ringing stops.

- **Protagonist and want:** Nadia wants to answer her brother's call.
- **Obstacle:** she holds herself back from speaking to him after years
  of estrangement. The ringing is the deadline, not the obstacle.
- **Immediate stakes:** this call may go unanswered. She could still
  call back; the story does not need to claim that every future chance
  disappears to make this choice matter.

## A summary, not a test

| Summary | Test |
|---|---|
| "A woman spends one last night in her mother's apartment." | "Nadia wants to answer her estranged brother's call but holds herself back after years of silence, risking a missed chance for contact when the ringing stops." |

Ask both sentences the same question: *so what happens if she doesn't get
it?* The summary has nothing to answer with — there's no stated objective
to fail. The test sentence answers immediately: this call goes unanswered
and their silence continues for now. A sentence that can't be asked
that question and answer it isn't a logline yet, whatever else it gets
right about tone or setting.

## Test the premise against the screen

A premise needs more than a small cast. Name what an audience will see
and hear, what changes, and how long the essential actions might take.
For "Before It Stops", the scale test is:

- **One location:** the kitchen of the mother's apartment. Boxes and bare
  shelves can establish the clearing without visits to other rooms.
- **One performer on screen:** Nadia. Theo is the caller; he does not
  need a separate location, reverse scene, or visible appearance.
- **Observable conflict:** the phone rings; she watches, reaches, stops,
  and withdraws. The estrangement is background pressure expressed
  through that behaviour, rather than a flashback explaining their past.
- **Visible change:** she finally accepts the call and says "Hey". The
  scene ends with an opening toward contact, without resolving their
  relationship. The following week's screenplay develops this beat.

Here is one **planning estimate**, targeting 2 minutes 10 seconds:

| Estimated screen time | Essential action | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–1:25 | Nadia clears the remaining kitchen objects, tapes the final box, and settles at the table with the phone nearby. | Establishes the last-night situation and a physical task reaching its end. |
| 1:25–1:55 | Theo's name appears; the phone rings while she reaches, stops, and withdraws. | Puts the want and obstacle into visible conflict under a deadline. |
| 1:55–2:10 | On the final ring she accepts, says "Hey", and listens. | Makes the change observable, then ends before a reconciliation scene is needed. |

These durations have **not been production-tested**. A timed enactment or
read-through must check whether the opening earns its length and the
hesitation stays legible. Do not stretch the ringing across the whole
runtime to fill time. If a section stalls, shorten or revise the action.
If explaining the premise requires more characters, locations, or time
than the limits allow, reduce its scope. In this example, a flashback or
a reconciliation conversation would add work beyond the immediate
decision the film is built around.

The question is not "can I write two minutes next to these actions?" It
is "does each action develop the pressure or the change within the
available time?" This is a scope check, before screenplay or shot design.

## What makes a short story good, not just filmable

Passing the objective/obstacle test and the scope test tells you a story
is testable and fits the runtime — it doesn't yet tell you the story is
any good. Short-form drama runs on one working assumption: the audience
gives you two to four minutes, and every one of those seconds has to earn
attention already, before any backstory can. Two things follow from that.

- **An emotional hook, not a premise recap.** The opening needs to put
  someone in a state the audience already feels something about — a
  decision they're delaying, a task they can't finish, a phone that won't
  stop ringing — not a scene that explains who everyone is before
  anything happens.
- **Avoid lengthy dialogue and abstract internal monologue.** Both cost
  runtime a short film doesn't have, and both tell the audience what a
  character feels instead of showing it. A short film earns its length by
  staying in observable behaviour: what a character does, not what they'd
  say about it if asked.

"Before It Stops" already makes this choice, even though nothing in its
premise states it explicitly. Nadia and Theo's estrangement is never
explained in dialogue — there's no line where she tells someone what
happened between them. It's carried entirely by behaviour: she watches
the phone, reaches, stops, withdraws. The emotional hook is the ringing
itself, not a monologue about what her brother did. If this premise
instead opened with Nadia narrating the history of their falling out, or
a phone call in which she explained her feelings at length, it would cost
the two minutes the film doesn't have, and would tell the audience the
feeling instead of letting them read it in what she does.

This is a filtering question, applied after the objective/obstacle and
scope tests, not a replacement for either: a logline can pass both of
those and still describe a story that leans on dialogue exposition or
interior narration it doesn't have room for.

## More loglines to test

The test works the same on any story you already know, not just this
course's teaching example:

- **Jaws**: *a police chief who's afraid of the water must stop a great
  white shark from killing more swimmers, over the mayor's refusal to close
  the beaches for tourist season.* Objective: stop the shark. Obstacle: not
  the shark alone — the mayor's refusal to close the beaches, which keeps
  putting swimmers back in the water.
- **Finding Nemo**: *after his son is taken by a diver, an anxious,
  overprotective clownfish must cross the open ocean to find him.*
  Objective: find and bring Nemo home. Obstacle: the ocean he's spent the
  whole film afraid of, and the overprotectiveness that caused the
  separation in the first place.
- **Die Hard**: *an off-duty cop must save his estranged wife and dozens of
  hostages from terrorists who've seized a Los Angeles skyscraper, alone
  and barefoot.* Objective: save the hostages. Obstacle: he's one person
  against an armed team, with no backup and no way out of the building.

Each one answers "so what happens if they don't get it?" immediately — the
swimmers keep dying, Nemo stays lost, the hostages don't make it out. That's
the same test, on stories you didn't have to be taught to already know.

## Guided judgement: what's missing?

A student brings this logline: *"A man returns to his childhood home and
confronts painful memories."* Before you check it against the test
question, name what's missing. There's a protagonist and a setting, and
something that sounds like an objective ("confronts painful memories" —
though that's closer to a mood than an action). But there's no obstacle:
nothing concrete stands between him and confronting those memories, so
nothing can actually go wrong. Fixed: *"A man returns to his childhood
home to finally clear it out, but every room forces him to decide what
to keep of a father he never forgave."* Now the obstacle is concrete — the
unresolved relationship, forcing a decision room by room — and the
question has something to answer.

## Synthesis

A logline is a claim with two testable parts: an objective and a concrete
obstacle. Character motivation — the thing a director's note keeps
pointing back to for the rest of this course — starts here: every note you
write later has to trace back to what your protagonist wants and what's
stopping them, exactly as this week's test checks for. The premise then
checks that the conflict can become observable action at a filmable scale.
The logline is the
fixed point every later week's work gets checked against; if it changes,
that has to happen on purpose, not by drift.
